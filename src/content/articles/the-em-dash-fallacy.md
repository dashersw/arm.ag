There’s a curious phenomenon brewing in digital discourse: the *em-dash fallacy*—the assumption that the presence of an em dash (—) in a piece of writing signals that it must have been generated by an AI model. It’s an odd superstition, as though a punctuation mark has suddenly become the watermark of artificiality.

But the em dash is not an invention of large language models. It predates them by centuries and is a legitimate, powerful element of written English. It offers a rhythm and flexibility no hyphen (-) or en dash (–) can replicate. It separates thoughts more intuitively than parentheses, more fluidly than colons, and more elegantly than commas. Writers from Emily Dickinson to David Foster Wallace have relied on it—not because they were machines, but because they were masters of tone.

Equating the em dash with AI betrays a kind of functional illiteracy—a confusion between unfamiliarity and inauthenticity. The real issue isn’t that LLMs use em dashes. It’s that many human writers haven’t yet learned to. Ironically, it’s the misuse of hyphens in place of em dashes that often reveals a lack of care in writing, not the other way around.

Let’s not throw out good grammar because machines use it. The em dash is here to stay—AI or not. And let’s not forget: AI learned the em dash from us, not the other way around.
