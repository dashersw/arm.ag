*Your MVP isn’t v1.0, it’s your scientific method. It's not always meant to scale, it’s meant to surface truth.* *Here’s the 90‑day plan for a better way to build an MVP.*

A lot of people have good ideas. Fewer people turn that idea into a product. And even fewer manage to build something people actually want, let alone love.

You don’t need more time. You don’t need another sprint. You don’t need to refactor your onboarding flow for the third time. What you need is a sharp understanding of what you’re building, why it matters, and how you’ll know if it’s working.

Over the past decade, I’ve worked with hundreds of early-stage teams, everyone from founders, PMs, developers, all chasing product/market fit. The pattern’s always the same: they jump straight into building.

Features get spec’d, backlogs fill up, GitHub repos are spun up within days. Weeks turn into months. Budgets burn. The first users arrive and bounce.

Why? Because the team skipped the hardest part: figuring out what to build in the first place.

Somewhere along the way, we glamorized shipping so much that “MVP” became synonymous with “buggy demo” or “barely functional beta.” But that was never the point. The original intent behind a Minimum Viable Product was to learn. It's a vehicle for reducing uncertainty, testing your riskiest assumptions, and validating demand with as little effort as possible.

Think of it like this: an MVP isn’t necessarily your version 1.0, it’s your scientific method. It's not always meant to scale, it’s meant to surface truth.

## A better way to build your MVP

Across roles as a CTO, founder, and product coach, I’ve seen this play out over and over. The most effective MVPs don’t try to do everything. They isolate variables, test hypotheses, and strip down scope until only the essentials remain.

That’s hard, especially for builders who want to show what they’re capable of . . . but building something that works is very different from building something that resonates.

A lot of first-time builders assume they need to do everything at once; design, backend, auth, analytics, onboarding. But your job isn’t to impress other devs. It’s to find proof.

That might mean:

- Asking better customer questions (and listening long enough to hear the answers)
- Running landing page tests to see if people click "Buy"
- Prototyping fake features to gauge demand
- Cutting 90% of your scope so the core idea actually gets seen

## Why this matters now

In 2025, the barrier to launching a product is basically gone.

AI copilots write your code. No-code tools scaffold your frontend. Infrastructure is serverless and instantaneous. You can go from idea to working prototype in a weekend and tens of thousands of people do exactly that, every week.

The upside of this is huge: we’ve never had more raw leverage as builders. One person can now do what used to take an entire team. The cost of experimentation has dropped to near zero. This is the best time in history to build something new.

But the downside is real too: the surface area of competition has exploded. You’re not just competing with startups anymore. You’re competing with everyone from solo hackers shipping polished tools in days to indie founders running a full stack with AI assistants. At this point, entire categories of software can (not saying should) be redefined or replaced by LLM-native agents.

Speed is the default now. Execution is commoditized. The new scarce resource is **clarity**. Knowing what to build, why it matters, and who it’s for is what I look for in the best MVPs.

The winners aren’t necessarily the best engineers or the most “innovative.” They’re the ones who get to signal first and who build something that speaks to a specific user's pain, in a way that feels inevitable after the fact. You hear about it, and it just makes sense.

If you can figure that out, meaning, if you can get to the truth faster than everyone else, then everything downstream becomes easier. Your messaging is clearer. Your roadmap shrinks. Your product gets traction. And you stop wasting time solving problems no one actually has.

Clarity compounds.
